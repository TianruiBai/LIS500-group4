About Us

This is our LIS 500 group project. We look at bias in technology, the people making it, and the different experiences of people using it.

Our group

Group members: Gisela Ines Rodriguez Garcia, and Tianrui Bai.

We choose to bring these topics together because technical performance is only part of the story. A website can load correctly and still be difficult for someone to use. A system can have a good average accuracy while making more mistakes for one group of people. Those differences matter when deciding whether the technology is actually doing its job.

How we use intersectionality

Intersectionality gives us a way to examine how different forms of inequality work together. Kimberlé Crenshaw's 1989 paper explains how treating race and sex as separate issues can leave out discrimination experienced by Black women.

Patricia Hill Collins and Sirma Bilge develop this approach in their 2016 book, Intersectionality. Race, class, gender, age, and disability do not operate independently in people's lives. The point is to study how these relationships affect access and power, rather than just list the identities a person has.

What this means for digital life

Consider an older adult with a low income and a visual impairment trying to use an online public service. Buying a newer device may be difficult. Small text and poor screen-reader support can make the service harder to use. If help is only available online, the same person has another barrier when something goes wrong. This is a hypothetical example, but it shows why providing an internet connection alone does not solve every access problem.

We think developers should account for these situations during design, instead of treating them as exceptions after release. That means checking who can afford to use a service, who is represented in its testing, and whether people have another way to get help. A single description of the "average user" is too limited for that work.

Where the other pages fit

The resource guide explains implicit bias, the IAT, and intersectionality in more detail. Our tech hero page looks at Shuji Nakamura's work on the blue LED and the conditions around his research. We chose him for his invention and earlier experience, including the practical work that came before the Nobel Prize.

Together, the pages connect technical work with access, recognition, and the decisions institutions make about people.
