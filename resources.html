Implicit Bias: A Resource Guide

Before discussing bias in software, it helps to explain what we mean by bias. We start with implicit bias, the Implicit Association Test (IAT), and intersectionality. There is quite a bit written about each topic, so this page keeps to the basics and a few examples. The links at the bottom are there for more detail.

Quick definitions

Implicit bias
Associations about people or groups that happen automatically, sometimes without us noticing. They can differ from what we consciously believe.
IAT (Implicit Association Test)
A sorting test that compares how quickly you respond to different pairings of words or pictures. The difference in time is used to estimate associations between concepts.
Intersectionality
A way of studying how race, gender, class, and other social categories interact in people's experiences of discrimination and power.

About implicit bias

A person can support equal opportunities and still picture a man first when thinking about an engineer. That automatic association is the kind of thing implicit bias describes. It can come from repeated experiences, stereotypes, or the people we see represented in media. Saying we believe in equality does not make those associations disappear.

This matters in tech because people still make the decisions behind it. Someone reviewing applicants might have a narrow idea of what a programmer looks like. A design team might treat its own needs as the needs of every user. These assumptions deserve attention even when nobody intended to exclude anyone.

Implicit Association Test

You can try the Implicit Association Test (IAT) on Project Implicit's website. It asks you to sort words or pictures using keys, then changes which categories share a key. The test compares your response times between the two arrangements. Being faster with one pairing is taken as evidence of a stronger association with that pairing.

What we think about the score

We don't think one IAT score should be used to label somebody. The result comes from a particular task, and treating it as a complete account of a person gives it too much weight. A few things matter when reading it:

  • The result can change. Practice, distraction, tiredness, or changes in associations can contribute to different scores on another attempt.
  • A score doesn't tell you what someone will do. One result is not a reliable prediction of one person's behavior in a specific situation.
  • The categories don't fit everyone. Some tests use pairs such as male/female. People's identities do not all fit into those two categories.

It is still worth including here as a way to learn about associations. But the explanation of the result matters as much as the number. Project Implicit's FAQ goes into the details, including disagreements about what scores mean.

Intersectionality

Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term "intersectionality," and scholars such as Patricia Hill Collins also contributed to this area of study. The idea is that discrimination connected to race, gender, class, or disability does not always happen separately. Studying one category at a time can leave out how they interact.

Take the experiences of Black women. A study of racism focused on Black men, combined with a study of sexism focused on white women, would not automatically explain those experiences. There are forms of discrimination involving race and gender together that this approach could miss.

The same problem comes up when testing software. Reporting results for "women" as one group can hide differences between women with different skin tones. Those differences need to be checked directly.

Bias in technology

A machine learning system learns from the data it is given. If that data represents some groups better than others, its performance can be uneven. Developers then choose how to test it and which results to report. Bias can be missed at either stage.

The Gender Shades study gives a concrete example. It tested commercial systems that assigned gender labels to images of faces, and found higher error rates for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. The task was gender classification, not identifying who a person was.

For us, reporting only the overall accuracy is not enough. A good average can hide poor results for a particular group. Developers should report those differences instead of leaving users to discover them after the software is put into use.
